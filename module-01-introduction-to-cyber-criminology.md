## 1. What is Cybercrime?

**In simple words:** Any crime where the internet, a computer, or a mobile phone is used in a wrong or illegal way is called cybercrime. In 2011, India recorded only about 1,100 cybercrime cases a year. By 2020, that number had crossed 54,000. The jump happened for a simple reason: almost every part of life - banking, shopping, chatting, studying - moved online, and criminals followed the crowd.

**Real-life example:** You get an SMS: 'Your bank account will be blocked, click this link to verify KYC.' You click, enter your details, and money disappears from your account. No thief entered your house, yet your money is gone. That one SMS is a cybercrime.

**Key points:**
- <b>Cyber security</b> means protecting information, devices, and networks from unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction.
- <b>Cyber law</b> means the legal rules for behaviour in cyberspace, mainly the internet. In India it is the Information Technology Act, 2000 (amended in 2008).
- Fewer than 11,000 companies in India work in cybersecurity, while attacks keep rising every year - the demand for skilled investigators is far bigger than the supply.

## 2. Five Common Cybercrime Examples

**In simple words:** Almost every cybercrime you hear about fits into five basic patterns. Learn these five and you can classify any new attack you come across:

**Real-life example:** None

**Key points:**
- <b>DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service):</b> flooding a website with fake traffic until it crashes. Like blocking a shop's entrance with a paid crowd so real customers cannot enter.
- <b>Malware:</b> malicious software - virus, worm, trojan horse, logic bomb. A digital infection that steals, damages, or spies.
- <b>Phishing:</b> fake emails, SMS, or login pages that look real and trick you into giving away passwords or OTPs.
- <b>Credential attack:</b> using stolen usernames and passwords to break into accounts - often trying one leaked password on many different sites.
- <b>Website hijacking:</b> secretly redirecting visitors of a real website to a fake lookalike site.

## 3. Types of Computer Crime: Type I vs Type II

**In simple words:** There are two big families. <b>Type I - the computer is the TARGET.</b> The criminal attacks the machine itself: hacking into it, infecting it, shutting it down, stealing its services. <b>Type II - the computer is the TOOL.</b> The criminal uses a computer to commit a traditional-style crime: fraud, fake content, harassment, stalking, money laundering.

**Real-life example:** Breaking into a college server and deleting records is Type I. Sitting on WhatsApp and running an OTP scam on strangers is Type II. Same phone, totally different crime family.

**Key points:**
- <b>Type I buckets:</b> unauthorised access (hacking - copying, modifying, or destroying data); malicious code (virus, worm, trojan horse, software bomb); interruption of services (disrupting or denying computer services); theft or misuse of services.
- <b>Type II buckets:</b> content violations (child abuse material, hate crimes, piracy, forgery of documents); fraud for personal or organisational gain (identity theft, online fraud, sabotage including critical infrastructure, share-market manipulation); improper use of communication (harassment, cyber stalking, extortion, phishing, drug trafficking, conspiracy).

## 4. Cybercrime in India: The Numbers

**In simple words:** Registered cases kept climbing every year: 27,248 in 2018, 44,735 in 2019, and 50,035 in 2020 - a jump of 11.8 percent in a single year. The crime rate touched 3.7 incidents per lakh population in 2020, up from 3.3 in 2019.

**Real-life example:** None

**Key points:**
- <b>Top reporting states (2020):</b> Uttar Pradesh 11,097; Karnataka 10,741; Maharashtra 5,496; Telangana 5,024; Assam 3,530.
- <b>Most reported types:</b> online banking fraud 4,047; fake news on social media 4,047; ATM-related cases 2,160; credit/debit card fraud 1,194; OTP frauds 1,093; cyber stalking 972; fake profiles 149; data theft 98.
- Globally, reports estimated over 500 million cyberattacks blocked in Q1 2023 alone, out of roughly a billion attacks - the scale is enormous, and most attacks never make the news.

## 5. How a Cybercrime Investigation Works

**In simple words:** Investigation is not a straight line - it is a wheel with five stages that keeps turning until the whole gang is caught, not just one person.

**Real-life example:** One mule account is caught. Its call records lead to three more numbers, their locations reveal a rented flat, and a raid there exposes the fake call centre. That is the wheel turning - one arrest feeding the next stage.

**Key points:**
- <b>Stage 1 - Primary data sourcing:</b> collect clues, informer tips, complaints, and daily crime inputs.
- <b>Stage 2 - Study and deeper collection:</b> read the data, cross-check police databases and open internet sources, confirm exactly what type of crime it is.
- <b>Stage 3 - Further investigation:</b> call detail records, lawful interception, tracking, location positioning, mapping the gang's structure and members.
- <b>Stage 4 - Arrest and evidence collection:</b> arrest suspects, seize computers and phones, check call records, booking records, and money trails.
- <b>Stage 5 - Follow-up:</b> use the first arrests to hunt connected targets and related evidence until the full network falls.

## 6. Reporting Cybercrime: The National Portal and I4C

**In simple words:** Any citizen can report a cybercrime at <b>www.cybercrime.gov.in</b>, and the national helpline is <b>1930</b>. Behind this sits <b>I4C - the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre</b> - which coordinates the fight against cybercrime across all states in one organised, collaborative system.

**Real-life example:** A student in Noida loses money to a fake part-time job scam. She files a complaint on the portal in ten minutes, the money trail is flagged the same day, and the case reaches her local cyber cell with a reference number. That pipeline is I4C working.

**Key points:**
- <b>The seven components of I4C:</b>
- 1. National Cybercrime Threat Analytics Unit (TAU) - studies threat patterns across the country.
- 2. National Cybercrime Forensic Laboratory (NCFL) - scientific examination of digital evidence.
- 3. National Cybercrime Training Centre (NCTC) - trains police officers and investigators.
- 4. Management of the cybercrime ecosystem - keeps all agencies working as one system.
- 5. Platform for joint investigation teams - officers from different states can investigate together.
- 6. National Cybercrime Reporting Portal - where citizens file complaints.
- 7. National Cyber Research and Innovation Centre - research on new threats and tools.
- Regional Cyber Crime Coordination Centres are also being set up in States and Union Territories.

## 7. Fundamentals of the Digital Crime Scene

**In simple words:** A crime scene is not only a room with a broken lock. When the crime happened on a phone or a server, the 'scene' is digital. The investigation logic is exactly the same as a physical scene, step for step: preserve, survey, document, search, reconstruct, present.

**Real-life example:** In a phishing case the 'scene' is the victim's phone, the fake website's server logs, and the email headers. If the victim factory-resets the phone in panic, the scene is destroyed - just like washing blood off a floor before the forensic team arrives.

**Key points:**
- <b>Physical model:</b> preserve the scene - survey for evidence - document everything - search - reconstruct what happened - present the complete theory.
- <b>Digital model mirrors it:</b> preserve the digital scene (isolate devices, stop auto-deletion) - survey for digital evidence - document - search logs, files, and metadata - reconstruct the digital sequence - present the digital theory.
- <b>Key difference:</b> digital evidence is invisible and fragile. One wrong click can overwrite it. So the first rule is always the same: preserve before you touch.

## 8. Cyber Threats and Identification

**In simple words:** <b>Threat detection</b> means spotting harmful activity on a network before it can exploit a weakness. <b>Threat response</b> means reacting fast enough to stop or limit the damage. No defence is perfect - strong security assumes the attacker might still get in, and plans for that worst case. Good detection mixes three things: skilled people, clear processes, and the right technology.

**Real-life example:** None

**Key points:**
- <b>Top 10 cybersecurity threats to know:</b>
- <b>Vulnerabilities</b> - newly found bugs (for example in mail servers) plus smarter phishing create new openings.
- <b>Business email compromise</b> - one hijacked mailbox used to send fraud mails in the company's name.
- <b>Crime-as-a-service</b> - readymade hacking tools and services sold in underground markets; even beginners can rent attacks.
- <b>Supply chain attacks</b> - poisoning trusted software updates so the malware arrives through a legitimate channel.
- <b>Cloud-based attacks</b> - misconfigured cloud storage and services are easy, high-value targets.
- <b>Data center attacks</b> - aimed at the facilities that house critical IT infrastructure.
- <b>Ransomware</b> - encrypts data and demands payment; delivery methods keep evolving.
- <b>IoT device hacking</b> - cameras, routers, and smart devices with weak passwords become entry points.
- <b>Insider threats</b> - a compromised employee account is more dangerous than an outsider, because it already has trust.
- <b>State-sponsored cyber warfare</b> - well-funded teams attacking for strategic or military goals.

## 9. Scams You Must Recognise

**In simple words:** Most scams reuse four or five old scripts. Once you know the script, the next call or mail cannot fool you:

**Real-life example:** None

**Key points:**
- <b>Call tag scam:</b> credit card fraud through fake 'wrong shipment' calls. The caller asks permission to 'pick up' a wrongly delivered item and harvests card details in the process.
- <b>Transaction account scam:</b> hack a company's email, then send the merchant a spoofed mail from the 'client' asking to pay into a 'new' account. The money is laundered through foreign accounts.
- <b>Bank account compromise:</b> change the victim's alert mobile number, or get a duplicate SIM issued by reporting the SIM 'lost'. Then quick ATM withdrawals and online purchases before the victim notices.
- <b>Stock market scam:</b> spam emails and chat groups pump a share's price (pump and dump / scalping), the gang sells fast at the peak and vanishes.
- <b>Looking ahead (2030 threat list):</b> supply chain compromise, advanced disinformation campaigns, digital surveillance and privacy loss, human error plus old unpatched systems, AI-enhanced targeted attacks, attacks on space infrastructure, advanced hybrid threats, cyber skill shortage, and AI abuse.

## 10. Corporate Frauds

**In simple words:** Corporate fraud is a web of deceit inside or against companies - and it costs far more than street crime. Reported figures show the scale: business email compromise alone caused about $676 million in losses, confidence and romance fraud about $211 million, and non-payment fraud about $141 million.

**Real-life example:** A finance manager gets a mail that looks exactly like the CEO's: 'Process a $250,000 wire transfer before close of business today.' It is not the CEO. One tired employee, one urgent mail - and the money is gone. That is business email compromise, the costliest corporate cyber fraud.

**Key points:**
- <b>Eight routes, one destination - loss of revenue and reputation:</b> bribery and corruption; hacking; supplier fraud; financial statement fraud; consumer fraud; theft and misuse of assets and information; insurance fraud; conflicts of interest.
- <b>Other heavy loss categories:</b> personal data breach ($77M), corporate data breach ($60M), identity theft ($66M), advance fee fraud ($57M), credit card fraud ($57M), real estate and rental fraud ($56M).

## 11. Frauds, Victims, and Vectors

**In simple words:** Every fraud needs three things: a <b>fraud</b> (the trick), a <b>victim</b> (the target), and a <b>vector</b> (the path the trick travels - a call, an SMS, a fake website, a malicious app). The list of fraud types is long because criminals copy whatever people trust: banks, government schemes, jobs, marriages, religion, investments, insurance, taxes.

**Real-life example:** A retired uncle gets a call: 'Sir, your pension needs re-verification, please share the OTP.' The trick is fake authority, the victim is chosen for trust, the vector is a phone call. Three pieces, one crime.

**Key points:**
- <b>What online adults report facing most:</b> impersonation fraud 51%; counterfeit goods scams 42%; investment or 'get rich quick' scams 40%; computer service or ransomware scams 37%; fake employment scams 30%; romance or dating scams 29%; identity fraud 24%; health or medical scams 24%; holiday scams 17%; money laundering 14%.
- <b>The lesson:</b> the top tricks all exploit trust, fear, greed, or urgency - never technology alone. That is why awareness is a security control.

## 12. How the Fraud Works: The Cyber Theft Ring

**In simple words:** Investigators mapped the full chain of an online banking fraud. It runs like an industry with departments - coders, exploiters, mules - stacked in a pyramid with victims at the bottom.

**Real-life example:** You install a 'bank KYC update' APK from a WhatsApp forward. It silently reads your SMS OTPs. At 2 AM your money hops through four mule accounts in ten minutes. By morning, the trail is cold and the app has vanished.

**Key points:**
- <b>The 8-step chain:</b>
- 1. A malware coder writes malicious software and sells it on the black market.
- 2. A victim's computer is infected with credential-stealing malware, usually a trojan.
- 3. Banking credentials are siphoned to a compromised collection server.
- 4. The hacker retrieves the stolen credentials.
- 5. The hacker gets remote access to the victim's machine through a compromised proxy.
- 6. The hacker logs into the victim's online bank account.
- 7. Money is transferred to money mule networks.
- 8. Mules forward the money to the organisers, keeping a small cut for themselves.
- <b>The pyramid:</b> malware coders at the top, then exploiters, then money mule networks, then victims at the bottom - individuals, businesses, and financial institutions.

## 13. Cyber Investigators: Who Fights Back

**In simple words:** Four kinds of people investigate cybercrime: <b>IT professionals, corporate security teams, private investigators, and law enforcement</b> - the police, where the final legal authority lies. The real question this module asks is: how can all four work together instead of in separate silos?

**Real-life example:** None

**Key points:**
- <b>The trends driving crime up:</b> internet and smartphone use keeps exploding; people put personal and work life online; money itself moved online; no security control is ever 100 percent; attackers usually stay one step ahead of defenders.
- <b>What motivates the criminal:</b> curiosity and revenge; ignorance of cyber law and its punishments; quick, unlawful money.
- <b>Protect yourself - the module's own checklist:</b> social media is now daily life, so be careful what you upload; use the privacy and security settings the platforms give you; SMS-based two-factor codes can be SIM-swapped, so prefer app-based authenticator codes; control who can see what; keep personal details personal.

## 14. Introduction to Cyber Forensics

**In simple words:** Cyber forensics - also called digital forensics - is the science of <b>collecting, analysing, and preserving digital evidence</b> so the truth behind a cyber incident can be proved. Just as traditional forensics solves physical crimes, cyber forensics solves digital ones: hacking, data breaches, fraud, and cyberattacks.

**Real-life example:** A suspect deletes his chat history. Forensics images the phone (acquisition), carves deleted records from free space (analysis), and the recovered messages become court evidence (reporting). Deleting is not the same as destroying.

**Key points:**
- <b>Nine branches:</b> digital forensics, system forensics, network forensics, web forensics, data forensics, proactive forensics, e-mail forensics, enterprise forensics, cyber forensics.
- <b>The 4-phase investigation process:</b> 1. Identification - find and check the evidence. 2. Acquisition - make a forensic copy; never analyse the original. 3. Analysis - examine the copy, recover deleted data. 4. Reporting - document everything and retain evidence for court.
- <b>The 7-step workflow:</b> data identification - project planning - data capture - data processing - data analysis - data display - report generation.
